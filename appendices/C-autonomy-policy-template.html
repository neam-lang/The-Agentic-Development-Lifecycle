Appendix C

Appendix C — Autonomy Policy Template

1722 words

An autonomy level enforced by instruction is not an autonomy level. It is a hope with a number attached.

Use this appendix to: write the artefact that makes an autonomy decision auditable — the thing Chapter 11 §11.9 argued accountability should attach to, instead of to an individual approval.

Established in: Chapter 13 (the ladder, the scoring instrument, revocations), Chapter 11 §11.9 (accountability), Chapter 16 §16.3 (the named owner), Chapter 21 (Autonomy Creep).

C.1 Why this document exists

Chapter 11 established that accountability once travelled with authorship. Authorship is now machine, so responsibility defaults to approval — precisely the gate Chapter 5 showed had collapsed. The result is that responsibility sits on the weakest remaining signal.

The way out is to attach accountability to the decision that actually determines outcomes: this class of change may run at this level of authority. That decision has to exist as a document for anyone to be accountable for it.

Three properties make it real rather than decorative:

It is per class of work, not per team. The same agent may run at A4 on dependency bumps and A0 on the payment path, in the same repository, in the same hour.

Every level is implemented as revoked capabilities. Chapter 13 §13.3. If the boundary lives in a context file, it is a belief-based control and Chapter 11 showed what those are worth under injection — or under ordinary misunderstanding.

The downgrade trigger is written while everyone is calm. Chapter 13 §13.9: a level granted with no stated conditions for revocation will never be revoked, because after an incident the conversation is about the incident rather than about whether the grant was sound.

C.2 The template

Copy, fill, commit next to the code it governs. One file per class of work, or one file with a section per class.

# AUTONOMY POLICY — <class of work>

Owner:            <name>            <!-- Ch 16 §16.3: harness owner -->
Approved by:      <name, role>
Date:             <YYYY-MM-DD>
Review date:      <YYYY-MM-DD>      <!-- 6 months maximum -->
Applies to:       <repository / path / change type>

---

## 1. Class of work

<One paragraph. What kind of change this covers, and — importantly —
what it does NOT cover. Ambiguity here is where Autonomy Creep enters.>

Examples in scope:      <2–3 concrete examples>
Explicitly out of scope: <2–3 concrete examples, with the class they
                          belong to instead>

C.3 Worked example

Filled in, for the two classes most organisations should write first — because they sit at opposite ends and make the instrument's point.

Class: dependency updates

Harnessability:  H1 2 · H2 1 · H3 1 · H4 2 · H5 1 · H6 1  = 8  (medium)
Blast radius:    B1 0 · B2 0 · B3 0 · B4 1 · B5 0 · B6 0  = max 1
Ceiling:         A3          Operating at: A4  ← ABOVE CEILING

⚠️ This is the most common real finding, and it is not a failure of nerve — it is Chapter 23's fourth crossing signal. The dependency bot has a schedule, which silently promoted it to A4 without anyone assessing whether it qualified. Either reduce B4 (pin, stage, canary) to reach blast 0, or drop the schedule.

Class: changes to the payment path

Harnessability:  H1 2 · H2 2 · H3 2 · H4 2 · H5 2 · H6 1  = 11 (high)
Blast radius:    B1 2 · B2 2 · B3 1 · B4 2 · B5 2 · B6 1  = max 2
Ceiling:         A0 / A1     Operating at: A2  ← ABOVE CEILING

Note what this shows. Harnessability is excellent — this is the best-instrumented code in the organisation. And the ceiling is still A0/A1, because blast radius vetoes (Chapter 13 §13.4). No amount of harness quality makes a severe-blast-radius change eligible for autonomy; you reduce the blast radius instead, or you accept the ceiling.

Together the two examples make the instrument's real payoff visible, and it is the one Chapter 13 §13.5 predicted: a uniform "A2 everywhere" policy is simultaneously above ceiling on the payment path and below it on dependency updates. The gain from scoring is not speed — it is reallocation of the scarce resource.

C.4 How this fails

Four failure modes, each from Chapter 21, each with its early sign.

The policy is written and the capabilities are not revoked. §5 is empty or aspirational. Early sign: you cannot name the mechanism in the "how it is enforced" column. This is the most common failure and it makes the whole document decorative.

No downgrade trigger. §6 left blank because nothing had gone wrong yet. Early sign: the review date passes and the policy is renewed without discussion.

Scope ambiguity. §1 says "backend changes". Early sign: two people disagree about whether a change is in this class, and resolve it in favour of the higher level. This is Autonomy Creep's entry point.

Nobody executes. §6's triggers fire and nothing happens because the named person moved teams. Early sign: the owner field has not been updated in two reorganisations.

Test it the way Chapter 13 §13.9 prescribes: attempt to exceed the level. Ask an A2 agent to merge. If it can, you are not at A2, whatever this document says — and that is the only verification that means anything.
